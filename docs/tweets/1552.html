KĀLĪ CHAUDAS: THE NIGHT OF THE DIVINE MOTHER AND THE AWAKENING OF THE SOUL
On Kālī Chaudas, as the night deepens and the moon disappears, we turn towards Mā Kālī. Her darkness is not the darkness of ignorance, but the mystery of the infinite, where all forms, colours and distinctions dissolve. This sacred night invites us to turn inwards, beyond the restless mind and the identities we have created, towards the eternal presence of God. Many are frightened by Mā Kālī’s fierce appearance: her dark complexion, flowing hair and garland of severed heads. Yet to her devotees she is the most loving of mothers. Her fierceness is compassion in its most powerful form. She destroys not her children, but everything that keeps them imprisoned in ignorance. She removes what is false so that we may discover what is eternal.
KĀLĪ AND THE MYSTERY OF TIME
Her name is associated with Kāla, time. Everything in this world eventually passes: youth, beauty, possessions, achievements, relationships and even our deepest sorrows. We spend our lives trying to hold on to what time will inevitably carry away. Mā Kālī reminds us that nothing temporary can provide permanent security. Her dark form symbolises the infinite reality into which all creation returns. She invites us to ask: Who am I beyond my body, my possessions, my memories and my identity? What remains when everything temporary falls away? This question is the beginning of spiritual awakening.
THE GARLAND OF HEADS AND THE DEATH OF EGO
Her garland can be contemplated as a symbol of the false identities we cling to: I am my success, my possessions, my anger, my suffering, my opinions. We mistake these passing experiences for our true self. The Mother takes away these masks, not to punish us, but to reveal who we really are. Her garland also carries a traditional association with the letters of the Sanskrit alphabet, symbolising the sacred sounds and powers of speech from which creation emerges. All names and sounds arise from the Divine and ultimately return to it.
THE VICTORY OF ŚRĪ KṚṢṆA
Kālī Chaudas also recalls Śrī Kṛṣṇa’s victory over Narakāsura and the liberation of the captives. Kṛṣṇa also defeated the demon Mura, earning the name Murāri. These divine victories invite us to recognise the inner battle against pride, greed, jealousy, anger, attachment and ignorance. Narakāsura can remind us of the arrogance that imprisons consciousness, while the liberated captives symbolise the freedom the soul seeks. Mā Kālī destroys the inner obstacles; Śrī Kṛṣṇa leads us towards liberation. Both remind us that we are not meant to remain prisoners of darkness.
THE SACRED ART OF STAYING AWAKE
The tradition of remaining awake on this night has a deeper meaning. It is possible to keep our eyes open while our consciousness remains asleep. True wakefulness is becoming aware of our thoughts, desires and actions rather than being controlled by them. The Bhagavad Gītā teaches: “What is night for all beings is the time of awakening for the self-controlled; and when all beings are awake, that is night for the wise sage.” (BG 2.69). The spiritual seeker learns to see beyond appearances and recognise the eternal reality that the world often overlooks. Tonight, let us sit quietly, observe our breath, chant the Divine Name and gently bring the wandering mind back to God.
THE GODDESS WITHIN ALL BEINGS
The Devī Māhātmya offers this beautiful salutation: “To the Goddess who dwells in all beings as consciousness, we bow again and again.” (5.20). Mā Kālī is not confined to the temple or an image. Her divine presence is celebrated as consciousness itself, the awareness through which we recognise our thoughts without becoming their prisoners. When we practise compassion, forgive, restrain anger and serve without expectation, our daily lives become expressions of devotion. Worship is not only what we offer before an altar; it is also how deeply the Divine transforms our conduct.
