Yama and Yamī
On Bhāī Dūj sisters invite their brothers home, put tilak on their foreheads and feed them with love. The day is also called Yama Dvitīyā. It remembers a brother and sister whose story begins in the Ṛg Veda. Yama and Yamī were twins, children of the Sun. A hymn of the Ṛg Veda (10.10) records a conversation between them. Yamī longs for a closeness that their bond as brother and sister cannot allow. We were made together in the womb, she says, so why should we ever be apart? Yama answers gently and firmly. Some bonds are sacred and must be honoured as they are. Then he speaks a line that has echoed for thousands of years: “They never stand still and they never close their eyes, the watchers of the gods who move among us.” (Ṛg Veda 10.10.8) Yama is remembered as the first in our tradition to choose dharma over desire. Yama and Yamī were among the very first beings and there were no rules yet to follow. With one quiet refusal, he drew the first boundary of dharma for all who would come after. Desire arrives with good reasons and a loving voice. Dharma looks further. It asks what our choice will do to the order that holds life together. Yama calls him Dharmarāja, the king of dharma. The Veda says he was the first mortal to walk the path of death and found the way for all who follow. The one who held to dharma in life became the guide for every soul crossing over. When we keep dharma in small things, we are preparing the path we will one day walk.
Then the story turns sweet. Yama died, the first of all beings to do so. Yamī could not stop grieving. Whenever the gods tried to comfort her, she said, “He died only today.” Time never moved for her to end her sorrow. So, night was created. With day and night, time began to move and slowly her grief softened. Even heaven understood that a broken heart needs time to heal. Later Yamī became the river Yamunā, flowing through Vraj where Kṛṣṇa played. Year after year she invited her brother to visit. At last, on the second day of the bright fortnight of Kārtika, he came. She welcomed him with a lamp, put tilak on his forehead and fed him with her own hands. Yama was so moved that he gave a blessing: any brother who visits his sister on this day and receives her love need not fear death. See how Yamī’s love had grown. At first it said, “Be one with me.” In grief it said, “I cannot let you go.” In the end it simply said, “Come, sit and eat.” Love that once wanted to possess learned to serve. This is the whole spiritual path in three moments, from longing through loss to selfless care. This is the closing jewel of Dīpāvalī. We have cared for the body, cleansed the heart, lit the inner flame and surrendered our burdens. Now we come home to a love that holds without clinging and serves without needing anything back. So on Bhāī Dūj, visit your siblings or call them if they are far.
