*The Physician and the Lord of Death*
Dhanteras brings two guests to our home. In the morning we remember Dhanvantri, the Divine Physician. In the evening we light a small lamp at the door for Yama, the Lord of Death. One carries a pot of elixir and the other carries a noose. It may seem strange to welcome both on the same day. Yet they have come to talk to us about the same thing. They have come to talk about this body we live in.Dhanvantri rose from the churning of the ocean with the pot of elixir in his hands. The Bhāgavatam counts him among the Lord’s own forms and Āyurveda honors him as its source. He arrives just before Lakṣmī Pūjā, as if to say gently that health is our first wealth. Kālidāsa put it in one simple line: The body is the first instrument of dharma (Kumārasambhava 5.33).
Think of how much this body has done for us. It has carried us through every year of our life. It has kept our heart beating through nights when we didn’t even think of it. Most of us thank it very rarely. An instrument needs care. A sitār left in the damp loses its tune. A body that is fed in a hurry, rested too little and pushed too hard can’t sit long in meditation or stay strong through a day of seva. Sometimes we spoil the body as if it were all we are. Sometimes we ignore it as if it didn’t matter. Dhanvantri asks us to be kind to it instead. It has been lent to us for a while and it’s how we chant, serve, bow and remember God. Then the sun goes down and Yama comes to the door. Most of us would rather not think of him. Yet our tradition lights him a lamp, turns it toward the south where he lives and asks us to remember him with love. So many of our fears grow from this one fear. We are afraid of losing the people we love, losing our health and losing our place in the world. Each of these small fears is a shadow of the last goodbye.
The Kaṭha Upaniṣad tells of a boy named Naciketa who went to Yama’s house and waited three days at his door. When Yama finally came, the boy asked him what happens when we die. Yama offered him gold, long life and kingdoms if he would only stop asking. Naciketa gently refused them all. Moved by his sincerity, Yama taught him:
“The Self is never born and never dies. It did not come from anything and it does not become anything. Unborn, eternal, everlasting and ancient, it is not destroyed when the body is destroyed.” (Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.2.18
How tender is this. The one who teaches us not to be afraid of death is Death himself. The body Dhanvantri heals will one day be laid to rest. The one who lives inside it was never born and will never die. Yama is not our enemy. He keeps the rhythm of time and reminds us that our days are numbered so that we will live them well. So the two guests belong together. Dhanvantri says, care for your body because it is precious. Yama says, don’t hold on too tightly because it won’t stay. We need to hear both. If we only care for the body, every grey hair and every doctor’s report fills us with worry. If we only think of death, we may stop caring for the life we have been given. Together they show us a gentle middle way. Keep the instrument in tune and remember who is playing it. Satguru reminds us that the soul’s relationship with God is eternal. This body is the place where we live that relationship in this life. When we know we are more than the body, we can care for it without fear. When we know we will one day leave it, we stop wasting the time it gives us. So this Dhanteras, eat something nourishing and thank God for every breath. Take the walk you keep putting off. Hold your loved ones a little longer. Then light a lamp at the door for Yama and greet him as a teacher. Each time we look at death with a lamp in our hand, the fear grows a little smaller and the love grows a little more.
