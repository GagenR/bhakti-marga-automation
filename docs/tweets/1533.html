When the Divine Calls on Śarad Pūrṇimā, Who Is Listening?*
_One full moon, two divine calls and a question for the human heart_
On Śarad Pūrṇimā two sacred traditions tell us the Divine is calling. In Vṛndāvana, beneath the autumn full moon, Kṛṣṇa plays His flute for the gopīs. In the Kojāgarī tradition, Mahālakṣmī walks through the world asking, Ko jāgarti? Who is awake? One is the call of divine love and the other the call of divine grace. The fullest moon of the year asks us something closer to home, whether we’ve learned to recognize the Divine in our own lives. Gopis were cooking and caring for their families when they heard the flute. They dropped everything because their hearts knew Him. A mother picks out her child’s cry among a hundred voices the same way. Love trains the heart to recognize what the ears might miss. Kṛṣṇa still calls us. His call reaches us through whatever turns our hearts back toward Him, a kindness we never expected that softens us or a longing that stays even after life has given us almost everything we wanted. Lakṣmī’s question gives Kojāgarī its name. On this night, she looks for those who are awake and blesses them. So, homes glow with lamps as everyone prays. Staying up all night can hardly be what grace is looking for, so being awake seems to point somewhere deeper. We receive a hundred kindnesses and yet remember only the one unkind word. We can spend years praying for God’s attention without seeing how little attention we’ve given Him.
The Kaṭha Upaniṣad (1.3.14) calls out, “Arise, awake and approach the wise to understand.” It speaks of awakening to spiritual truth, yet it could almost be Lakṣmī’s own question, asking us to wake from comparison and endless wanting to the love we already take for granted.
Devotees pray to Mahālakṣmī for abundance on this night and there’s nothing wrong with that. A comfortable home, good health and the means to care for those we love are worth praying for. Yet one of her greatest gifts may be the ability to feel we have enough. Working joyfully toward a better life is very different from believing we can’t be happy until we get there, and her grace sometimes arrives as what we asked for and sometimes as the discovery that we no longer need it.The two calls are less different than they seem. Kṛṣṇa draws us through love while Mahālakṣmī asks us to wake up to what we’ve been given. Love depends on that wakefulness, since we can’t truly love someone while staying absorbed in ourselves. We tend to think of devotion as more prayers, more chanting and more rituals, yet beneath all of it lies the willingness to make room for God. We ask for His peace while holding on to old resentments, ask Him to come closer and can’t spare a few quiet moments simply to be with Him. The gopīs never needed persuading because their hearts already belonged to Him.The moon that rises on this night holds both calls in its light. In Hindu thought it’s tied to the mind, which waxes and wanes just like it, full of faith one day and clouded by doubt the next. Yet the moon has no light of its own. It shines only because it faces the sun and on Śarad Pūrṇimā it faces the sun so completely that every part of it is lit. The gopīs turned towards the flute in the same way, while Lakṣmī’s blessing comes to whoever is turned toward her when she passes. Neither the moon nor the devotee produces the light. Grace is entirely His to give. What we can do is face Him. The moon knows it is not a sun and does not try to become one. It stays as itself while becoming radiant through that relationship. We don’t have to stop being ourselves to belong completely to Him and as we receive His love some of it starts to shine through the way we live.
Tonight, before we ask for one more blessing, we can stop and count the ones we’ve been walking past.
